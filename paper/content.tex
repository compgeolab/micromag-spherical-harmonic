\section{Introduction}

Recent advances in magnetic microscopy have expanded its potential for quantitative paleomagnetic applications by enabling the investigation of increasingly complex magnetic sources. Magnetic microscopy will continue to push toward smaller sensor–sample standoff and higher spatial resolution, revealing complex magnetic behavior beyond dipole-dominated signatures. Under these conditions, higher-order contributions (quadrupole, octupole, and beyond) become non-negligible \citep[\textit{e.g.,}][]{CortesOrtuno2021, CortesOrtuno2022, Bellon2026}, particularly for larger grains, clustered sources, and measurements close to the sample. Although the dipolar contribution can alone be used to efficiently describe the full magnetic moment of a source \citep{Bellon2026}, it is the non-dipolar components of a stray field that will carry a particle's domain-state signature \citep{Fabian2019,CortesOrtuno2022}. Spherical–harmonic (SH) expansions \citep{CortesOrtuno2022,Lima2023} provide a compact representation of fields by expressing the external magnetic potential on a virtual spherical surface enclosing the source.

In magnetic microscopy,  \citet{Lima2023} showed that it is possible to use surface-plane measurements — e.g., the vertical component of the magnetic field of a thin section acquired with a Quantum Diamond Microscope (QDM) — to invert the magnetic moment of a given source using SH expansions. It is well known that the degree 1 SH coefficients under Schmidt normalization are proportional do the three components of a magnetic dipole located at the expansion center \citep{Blakely1996}. On planar data, however, the spherical-harmonic coefficients are determined conditionally on the geometry and assumptions of the inversion. A single planar map samples only a limited, upper-side angular aperture of the external field and therefore does not preserve the spherical-harmonic orthogonality available on a closed enclosing surface. As a result, the fitted coefficients can become correlated and sensitive to measurement noise, model truncation, the assumed expansion center, and the spatial aperture of the measurements. In particular, errors in the expansion center can redistribute power among spherical-harmonic degrees, potentially introducing artificial higher-order contributions and biasing lower-order coefficients \citep{Lima2023}.

These issues could be mitigated, as shown by \citet{Out2026}, by using double-sided magnetic microscopy combined with three-dimensional tomographic experiments to determine the positions of magnetic sources within the sample's subsurface. However, incorporating tomography into the experimental workflow introduces additional complexity and cost. Conventional computerized CT scans often lack the resolution to detect the smallest single-domain (SD) and single-vortex (SV) particles. Even when high-resolution techniques do succeed, they are often chemically or structurally unresolved (e.g., Focused Ion Beam tomography, \cite{Nikolaisen-2020,Hristo-2025}), or they must be restricted to very small areas (typically on the order of $\sim 20~\mu\mathrm{m}^2$), as is the case for ptychographic X-ray computed nanotomography \citep{Bellon-2024a}. 

An alternative approach is to rely solely on the observed magnetic data and invert SH coefficients only for the detected anomalies, which represents a virtual source enclosed within a single shell centered on that anomaly. Unlike gravitational potential data, for which the center of mass provides a natural reference point for spherical expansions, magnetic data lack an equivalent and uniquely defined ``magnetic center'' \citep{Lima2023}. This creates a major practical challenge: under the planar geometry of magnetic microscopy, even small source-centering errors may induce spectral leakage and bias the recovered SH coefficients and dipole moment.
% Under this open-geometry configuration, even small source-centering errors may generate severe spectral leakage, redistributing power among harmonic degrees, coupling coefficients, and biasing lower-order estimates such as the dipole moment.

In this context, we pursue a center-of-expansion seeding technique based on Euler deconvolution (ED) \citep{Reid1990,Souza-Junior2024,SouzaJunior2026}. The method requires only the observed magnetic field and its derivatives, avoids explicit size and shape assumptions, and is well conditioned for local planar problems. In parallel, we implement the SH formulation of \citet{Lima2023}, enabling a spectrally complete representation of the magnetic field that can be propagated to arbitrary observation planes once the expansion center and truncation degree are properly constrained. We apply this methodology to both realistic synthetic and real QDM datasets and compare it against dipole-based models from \citet{SouzaJunior2026}. The synthetic dataset consists of idealized micromagnetic particle models, which allow us to delineate the conditions under which the method performs best as a function of sensor height, grain size and shape, and noise level. The real data enable an assessment of robustness under practical measurement conditions.

\section{Methods}

The general methodology for inversion of magnetic microscopy data follows \citet{Souza-Junior2024} and \citet{SouzaJunior2026}. A summary of the workflow is as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Calculate the $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-derivatives of the data.
    \item Calculate the total-gradient amplitude \citep{Blakely1996} and stretch its contrast to highlight weaker sources.
    \item Detect the individual magnetic signals using a Laplacian of Gaussian method \citep{Kong2013}.
    \item For each detected signal:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Isolate the data for that source.
        \item Estimate the position of the source using Euler Deconvolution \citep{Reid1990}.
        \item Estimate the magnetic moment through a linear inversion.
        \item Perform a non-linear inversion to refine the source position and magnetic moment estimates.
        \item Forward model the signal and remove it from the entire dataset.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

The main change introduced here to the methodology above is the replacement of the linear and non-linear dipole inversions with spherical harmonic counterparts to account for multipolar components in the data.

\subsection{Spherical–harmonic inversion of $b_z$}

Based on the conventional spherical-harmonic (SH) representation \citep{Lima2023}, we construct an SH expansion that reproduces the measured vertical field component $b_z$ on the observation surface. The exterior SH expansion is valid at any observation point outside a sphere enclosing all magnetic sources. However, the standard orthogonal recovery of SH coefficients requires field sampling over a complete enclosing spherical surface. For single-plane measurements, the expansion remains valid as a forward model, but its coefficients must be estimated by a regularized inversion and can be correlated because the SH basis is not orthogonal over the sampled aperture. Here we propose an alternative and more flexible strategy to choose the expansion center. Following \citet{Souza-Junior2024} and \citet{SouzaJunior2026}, we use Euler Deconvolution \citep{Reid1990} to estimate the location of the source under a dipolar approximation, which is then used as the expansion center $\mathbf{p} = (x_c, y_c, z_c)$.

We first define a Cartesian system $(x, y, z)$ that is aligned with the magnetic microscopy image, with the $z$-axis pointing upward. The origin of the system is the lower left corner of the sample. Given an expansion center at $(x_c, y_c, z_c)$, we can define a spherical coordinate system $(r, \theta, \phi)$ with origin on the expansion center (Figure \ref{fig:coordinates-system}). Here, $r$ is the radial coordinate, $\theta$ is the colatitude (angular distance from the $z$-axis), and $\phi$ is the longitude or azimuth (angular distance from the $x$-axis). The spherical harmonic expansion of the vertical component of the magnetic field $b_z$ is

\begin{figure}[tb!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/coordinate-system-sketch.png}
\caption{Schematic diagram of the measurement geometry and coordinate systems. The Cartesian coordinate system $(x, y, z)$ is established with its origin at the sample surface ($z = 0$). Measurement data points (blue dots) are acquired on a planar grid at a constant elevation ($z > 0$) above the surface. An expansion center is located at depth within the sample volume, defining a translated local coordinate system $(x', y', z')$. The red vector $\vec{m}$ represents the source vector (e.g., dipole moment) at this center. The spatial relationship between the expansion center and an arbitrary data point is described by the position vector $\mathbf{r}$ and the spherical angles $\theta$ (colatitude) and $\phi$ (longitude).
}
\label{fig:coordinates-system}
\end{figure}

\begin{equation}
    b_z(r,\theta,\phi) = \mu_0 \sum_{n = 1}^{L} \left(\frac{a}{r} \right)^{n+2}
    \sum_{m = 0}^{n}
    \left[
        (n + 1) P_n^m(\cos\theta)\cos\theta
        + \dfrac{\partial P_n^m}{\partial\theta}(\cos\theta)\sin\theta
    \right]
    \left(
        g_n^m\cos m\phi + h_n^m\sin m\phi
    \right)
\ ,
\label{eq:sh}
\end{equation}

\noindent in which $\mu_0$ is the vacuum magnetic permeability, $n$ is the degree, $m$ is the order, $L$ is the maximum degree of the expansion, $a$ is the reference radius, $P_n^{m}$ are the Schmidt-normalized associated Legendre functions, and $g_n^m$ and $h_n^m$ are the Gauss coefficients of the expansion. The $P_n^m$ and their derivatives are calculated with recursion relations \citep{Holmes2002}, as implemented in the \textit{Harmonica} library \citep{harmonica2020}.

Defining a observed data vector $\mathbf{d}^o$ containing the $N$ observations of $b_z$ and a parameter vector $\mathbf{c}$ as

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{c} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}    
        g_1^0 & g_1^1 & g_2^0 & g_2^1 & \cdots & g_L^L 
        & h_1^1 & h_2^1 & h_2^2 & h_3^1 & \cdots & h_L^L
    \end{bmatrix}^T_{M \times 1} \ ,
\end{equation}

\noindent
we can formulate the forward problem in Equation~\ref{eq:sh} as a linear system $\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{G}\mathbf{c}$. The Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{G}$ has columns corresponding to the cosine- and sine-type basis functions associated with the Gauss coefficients $g_n^m$ and $h_n^m$ and is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{G} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        K_{1, 1}^0 & K_{1, 1}^1 & K_{2, 1}^0 & K_{2, 1}^1 & \cdots & K_{L, 1}^L & J_{1, 1}^1 & J_{2, 1}^1 & J_{2, 1}^2 & \cdots & J_{L, 1}^L
        \\
        K_{1, 2}^0 & K_{1, 2}^1 & K_{2, 2}^0 & K_{2, 2}^1 & \cdots & K_{L, 2}^L & J_{1, 2}^1 & J_{2, 2}^1 & J_{2, 2}^2 & \cdots & J_{L, 2}^L
        \\
        & & & & & \vdots 
        \\
        K_{1, N}^0 & K_{1, N}^1 & K_{2, N}^0 & K_{2, N}^1 & \cdots & K_{L, N}^L & J_{1, N}^1 & J_{2, N}^1 & J_{2, N}^2 & \cdots & J_{L, N}^L        
    \end{bmatrix}_{N \times M} \ ,
\label{eq:jacobian}
\end{equation}

\noindent
in which $K_{n,i}^m$ and $J_{n,i}^m$ are

\begin{align}
K_{n, i}^m &= \left( \frac{a}{r_i} \right)^{n + 2} \left[
    (n + 1) P_n^m(\cos\theta_i)\cos\theta_i + 
    \dfrac{\partial P_n^m}{\partial \theta}(\cos\theta_i)\sin\theta_i
\right] \cos m\phi_i \ ,
\\
J_{n, i}^m &= \left( \frac{a}{r_i} \right)^{n + 2} \left[
    (n + 1) P_n^m(\cos\theta_i)\cos\theta_i + 
    \dfrac{\partial P_n^m}{\partial \theta}(\cos\theta_i)\sin\theta_i
\right] \sin m\phi_i \ .
\end{align}

The parameter vector $\mathbf{c}$ is then obtained through a damped linear least-squares inversion (zero-order Tikhonov regularization). This approach estimates $\mathbf{c}$ by minimizing the objective function

\begin{equation}
\Phi(\mathbf{c}) = \| \mathbf{d}^o - \mathbf{G}\mathbf{c} \|_2^2 + \lambda \| \mathbf{c} \|_2^2
\end{equation}

\noindent
in which $\lambda$ is the positive regularization parameter. However, the columns of the Jacobian $\mathbf{G}$ can have very different scales as the degree $n$ increases, leading to instability of the inverse problem. To counteract this, we normalize the Jacobian matrix using a diagonal matrix $\mathbf{S}$, whose diagonal elements $S_{ii} = \sigma_i$ are the standard deviation of the $i$-th column of $\mathbf{G}$ \citep{Soler2021}. The objective function is transformed to 

\begin{equation}
\Phi(\mathbf{w}) = \| \mathbf{d}^o - \underbrace{\mathbf{G}\mathbf{S}^{-1}}_{\mathbf{D}}\underbrace{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{c}}_{\mathbf{w}} \|_2^2 + \lambda \| \mathbf{w} \|_2^2
\end{equation}

The optimal normalized coefficient vector $\mathbf{w}$ is obtained by solving the corresponding normal equations

\begin{equation}
(\mathbf{D}^T \mathbf{D} + \lambda \mathbf{I})\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{D}^T \mathbf{d}^o
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $I$ is the identity matrix. Once an estimate of $\mathbf{w}$ is obtained, we calculate the spherical harmonic Gauss coefficients as $\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{w}$.

Another advantage of the normalization of the Jacobian matrix is that the range of acceptable values for the regularization parameter $\lambda$ will be more predictable since it won't depend on the model and data dimensions as strongly. Here, we use a regularization parameter of $\lambda = 10^{-1}$, which was empirically selected as the value that best preserves the benchmark results obtained from the micromagnetic particle simulations (see Section~\ref{numerical_simulation}).

Once the Gauss coefficients have been estimated, the Cartesian components of the magnetic moment (in $Am^2$) of a dipolar source located at the expansion center $(x_c, y_c, z_c)$ are calculated directly from the first-degree coefficients. Following \citet{Blakely1996},

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{m} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    m_x \\ m_y \\ m_z
\end{bmatrix} = 4\pi \mu_0 a^3
\begin{bmatrix}
    g_1^1 \\ h_1^1 \\ g_1^0
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\noindent
in which $a$ is the reference radius that contains the magnetic source. For our purposes, it is safe to assume that the source is contained in a circle of radius equal to the minimum radial coordinate $r_i$ of the data.


\subsubsection{Nonlinear Position Refinement}\label{nonlinear-inversion}

The estimated Gauss coefficients and the corresponding magnetic moment $\mathbf{m}$ are strongly dependent on the choice of expansion center $(x_c, y_c, z_c)$. 
It is also know that Euler Deconvolution is sensitive to random noise in the data and the presence of interfering sources in the data window \citep{Uieda2025,Silva2001}.
Thus, the estimated magnetic moment based solely on the Euler Deconvolution and spherical harmonics results will likely be biased.

To refine the estimate, we employ a non-linear inversion method similar to what \citet{SouzaJunior2026} did for dipole inversion.
We refine the expansion center estimate by minimizing the mean-squared error of the $b_z$ prediction using the Nelder-Mean derivative-free optimization method \citep{Nelder-Mead1965, 2020SciPy-NMeth}. At each candidate center, the spherical coordinates $(r,\theta,\phi)$ of all data points are recomputed, the design matrix $G$ is rebuilt, and the coefficients are re-estimated. This refinement is more computationally demanding, but it substantially improves stability and accuracy when the sources are clustered in the microscopy image or are asymmetric. The Euler Deconvolution and linear spherical harmonic inversion results are used as initial estimates for the non-linear Nelder-Mead optimization.


\subsection{Performance metrics}\label{metrics}
% To quantify the performance of each inversion strategy, three misfit metrics were used.  

To enable a systematic comparison between the tested inversion strategies for the synthetic models, a set of complementary misfit metrics was adopted to evaluate the recovery of the main source parameters. These metrics quantify the accuracy of the estimated magnetic moment direction and intensity and the source position relative to their known true values. The directional accuracy was measured by the angular misfit: 

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ang_misfit}
\Delta\alpha~[^{\circ}] = \arccos\!\left(
\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}\cdot \mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{true}}}
{\|\hat{\mathbf{m}}\|\,\|\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{true}}\|}
\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\hat{\mathbf{m}}$ denotes the recovered dipole-moment vector, $\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{true}}$ the corresponding true dipole-moment vector. The relative dipole-moment misfit quantified the intensity accuracy: 

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:m_misfit}
\Delta m~[\%] =
100\,\frac{\big|\|\hat{\mathbf{m}}\| - \|\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{true}}\|\big|}
{\|\mathbf{m}^{\mathrm{true}}\|},
\end{equation}

 \noindent Finally, we assessed the position misfit, defined as the Euclidean distance between the true source centroid $\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{true}}$ and recovered expansion center coordinates $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = (x_c, y_c, z_c)$:


\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pos_misfit}
\Delta r~[\mu m] = \left\|\hat{\mathbf{p}} - \mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{true}}\right\|.
\end{equation}




\section{Synthetic Model}\label{numerical_simulation}

In this section, we evaluate the effectiveness of the SH method using a simulated realistic dataset of idealized shaped sub-micron magnetite ($Fe_3O_4$) particles. A total of 20 particle models were considered, comprising four shape classes: spheres, prolate and oblate ellipsoids, and euhedral cuboctahedral particles. For the spherical particles, the radius ranged from 0.025 to 0.5~$\mu$m. Prolate ellipsoids semi-axes were defined with $a > b = c$, while oblate ellipsoids follow $a = b > c$, with semi-axis ratios of 2:1 between the major and minor axes. In both cases, the characteristic size was varied over the same range as for the spheres radii. For the euhedral cuboctahedral particles, geometric parameters are controlled by a maximum elongation parameter $E_{\mathrm{max}}$, which varies from 0.05 to 1.

To compute the stray magnetic fields, we first determined the Local Energy Minimum (LEM) domain states for each particle using the open-source micromagnetic solver \textsc{MERRILL} \citep[Micromagnetic Earth Related Robust Interpreted Language Laboratory,][]{Oconbhui-2018}. Each particle geometry was discretized into linear tetrahedral finite elements, and the total magnetic free energy was minimized. For every particle, 100 independent minimizations were performed, and the thermodynamically most probable configuration (i.e., the lowest-energy state) was selected as the representative LEM. We obtained a variety of domain-states, in a way that we observe a transition from single-domain (SD), to single-vortex (SV), up to multivortex (MV) as the particle sizes increase. The forward stray field was then computed using a tetrahedral integration approach \citep{Bellon2026}, adapted from \citet{Fabbri-2008}. This method exploits the tetrahedral discretization used in \textsc{MERRILL} by evaluating the magnetic field contribution of each uniformly magnetized element based solely on its geometry and magnetization direction.

Before applying the SH framework to planar observations, we evaluated its performance under ideal conditions by fitting the micromagnetically predicted stray field on a spherical shell enclosing each particle. The shell was defined at a constant radial offset from the particle, corresponding to 5\% of its largest semi-axis, and only the vertical component $b_z$ was considered to remain consistent with QDM measurements. In this configuration, the field is sampled over a closed surface, satisfying the orthogonality conditions required for spherical harmonic expansions. 

SH fits up to degree 10 reproduce the synthetic stray field with negligible residuals (Figure~\ref{fig:spherical-harmonic-fitting}), with no significant improvement observed for higher degrees. A damping factor of $10^{-1}$ was adopted, as it provides a stable solution while preserving the fidelity of the recovered coefficients. Together, this choice of maximum degree and damping was found to yield consistent results in benchmark tests, in which the dipole moment estimated from the SH coefficients agrees with the total magnetic moment computed directly from the micromagnetic models (i.e., the sum of the $m_x$, $m_y$, and $m_z$ contributions over all tetrahedral elements from \textsc{MERRILL}). This demonstrates that the resulting coefficients accurately capture the natural spatial decay of the field and provides a reference benchmark for subsequent analyses on planar data, where these ideal conditions are not met.

\subsection{Micromagnetic benchmarking: dipolar \textit{vs} multipolar approaches}\label{approaches-comparison}

To compare the effectiveness of the SH approach in a magnetic microscope setting, the stray field of all the 20 particles was predicted on a two-dimensional grid with sensor--source distances ranging from 0.05 to 10.0~$\mu$m, sampled at discrete heights. We evaluate the performance of dipolar and spherical harmonic inversion approaches, considering both linear and nonlinear formulations, across a range of particle geometries and characteristic scales. The dipolar models followed the approaches presented by \citet{Souza-Junior2024} and \citet{SouzaJunior2026}, respectively, for linear and nonlinear cases.

The analysis includes the aforementioned geometries with variations in effective size, allowing a systematic assessment of model robustness under increasing geometric complexity, although without noise. To quantify the inversion accuracy by benchmarking against the reference micromagnetic model, we evaluated the angular, magnetic moment, and spatial position misfits (see Section~\ref{metrics}). 

%Colors denote the inversion strategy, and marker styles indicate particle size or aspect ratio.

Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-micromag-model} summarizes the results. Each column corresponds to a different particle geometry or scaling condition, while rows represent distinct metrics used. A consistent pattern across all configurations is the improved performance of nonlinear inversions relative to their linear counterparts. Nonlinear methods yield results closer to the reference values, indicating a more accurate recovery of source properties. This behavior is particularly evident in the directional and intensity misfits, where linear solutions exhibit larger variability and systematic deviations. Comparing model parameterizations, spherical harmonic approaches exhibit greater stability than dipolar approximations, particularly for sensor heights near the particles. While the performance of dipolar models degrades significantly for near-superficial, elongated, and/or flattened geometries. Both approaches provide reasonable estimates for distal sources, considering the attenuation of high-order multipole effects.

%This degradation manifests as systematic biases rather than increased random scatter, indicating that the errors are primarily driven by model inadequacy rather than noise.

The influence of particle geometry is clearly observed across all panels. As the aspect ratio increases, both dipolar and SH solutions exhibit larger misfits. Although the effect is substantially more pronounced for dipolar models. In contrast, SH inversions maintain relatively stable behavior, reflecting their greater flexibility in representing more complex magnetic field distributions. Especially in the SH nonlinear, which yielded lower values for angular and magnetic moment misfits, where most results for all geometries and grain sizes fall below the 1\textdegree/1\% threshold (dashed black line). The lower panels (Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-micromag-model}) reveal systematic trends in the position misfit that further highlight differences between methods. These results mostly remained under the $1.0~\mu m$ across all approaches, except for the two largest grain sizes, for which the position error increased proportionally with sensor height. On the other hand, for the SH nonlinear approach these higher misfits in position showed no significant negative influence in both directional and intensity estimations.

Overall, the results demonstrate that the inversion strategy play a critical role in accurately recovering magnetic source properties. Nonlinear approaches are more consistent. However, only the SH counterpart showed reliable estimates across all tested scenarios, while nonlinear dipolar models are prone to significant systematic errors at near particles surface distances, and when applied bigger and/or non-idealized source geometries (prolates and oblates).

\subsection{Evaluation of noise level effects}

Simulating the noise level is one of the key pieces needed to complete the puzzle of a ``realistic scenario''. Since the nonlinear approaches yielded more stable solutions (see Section~\ref{approaches-comparison}), these methods were applied using two distinct fixed noise levels \citep[$20$ and $100~nT$, both values reported in][]{Glenn2017}. Instead of the realistic QDM noise described by \citet{Bellon2026}, high-frequency zero-mean Gaussian noise was adopted, as the window size of the data is much smaller than a typical QDM field of view. Then, the fixed-noise forward models were calculated for the intermediate particle size of each modeled geometry: sphere ($R=0.250~\mu m$), prolate ($a=0.250 \neq b=c=0.125~\mu m$), oblate ($a=b=0.250 \neq c=0.125~\mu m$), and euhedral ($E_{max}=0.5~\mu m$). 

The results are summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-micromag-model-with-noise}. Each column corresponds to a different particle geometry, while the rows represent distinct misfit metrics. The simulations show consistent behavior for both directional and intensity misfits: for sensor heights $\leq 1~\mu m$, the SH approach performs significantly better than the dipolar model. In some cases, SH results at higher noise levels ($100~nT$, solid red line) are more accurate than those for the dipolar approach at lower noise levels ($20~nT$, dashed blue line). This discrepancy decreases markedly for heights $\geq 1~\mu m$ as the higher-order signals are attenuated, but only over a limited range (around $2~\mu m$). As the distance increases further, magnetic field attenuation becomes dominant, causing the fixed noise level to be too high for reliable results. The dotted vertical lines indicate the height at which the noise exceeds 10\% of the maximum magnetic anomaly amplitude. This threshold also marks the point beyond which both approaches yield unreliable results due to a lower signal-to-noise ratio. The position misfits remained nearly identical for both approaches across the full range of heights.

\section{Real Data Application}

After assessing the reliability of the SH approach with the realistic synthetic scenario, the next step involves the application in real magnetic microscopy measurements. The dataset used in this section was obtained using the Ensaio software \citep{Ensaio2025}. It consists of a high-resolution magnetic map of a stalagmite sample from Wintimdouine Cave, Morocco \citep{carmo2019speleothem}. The data were collected to investigate the magnetic remanence properties of hematite and magnetite within the sample.
The isothermal remanent magnetization (IRM) of the sample was mapped using a QDM at the Harvard Paleomagnetics Lab, Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences, Harvard University. The IRM was imparted by applying magnetic fields of 2.7 T in the $+y$ direction and 0.3 T in the $-y$ direction. The QDM data were measured with a bias magnetic field of 0.9 mT, which was applied during the measurements and periodically reversed, and a background field of approximately 400 nT, as described by \citet{Hess2024}. The data were acquired in projected magnetic microscopy (PMM) mode and converted to the vertical component of the magnetic field ($b_z$) using a spectral approach \citep{Fu2020, Glenn2017, Lima2009}, which was processed with the aid of the \textit{QDMLab} \textit{software} \citep{Volk2022}. The imaged area covers $1410~\mu m \times 2256~\mu m$, with a grid spacing of $2.35~\mu m$, corresponding to approximately $5.76 \times 10^5$ measurement points. Data acquisition was conducted at a constant sensor–sample distance of $5~\mu m$, enabling high-resolution mapping of the magnetic field distribution. 

A slice of the QDM data was selected for comparison between both nonlinear approaches, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-real-data}. The data processing used to estimate the magnetic moment vector of each identified anomaly followed the workflow presented by \citet{SouzaJunior2026}, which consists of semi-automatic anomaly window selection, nonlinear inversion of the anomaly source, and forward modeling of its signal to subtract its influence from less pronounced neighboring anomalies. The same workflow was subsequently applied to the nonlinear SH approach. 

Since the data were acquired using QDM at a sensor height of $5~\mu m$ (Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-real-data}a), substantial differences between the approaches are not expected. At this distance, higher-order signal contributions are already strongly attenuated for most particles, so the measured field is predominantly controlled by the lower-order terms, except in the case of larger grains \citep{Bellon2026}. This expectation is consistent with the results, as both approaches produced similar estimates and residual maps (Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-real-data}c and f) and magnetic moment (Figure~\ref{fig:comparison-real-data}d). However, minor discrepancies were observed, either for the larger anomaly, where residual higher-order contributions occurred, or for weaker anomalies that were highly affected by background magnetic shifts, noise levels, or influenced by the stringer sources. These conditions may still influence the inversion.

\section{Discussions}

By incorporating higher-order magnetic moments, multipole fitting provides additional degrees of freedom to capture the magnetic complexity of remanence carriers. This approach is particularly valuable in high-resolution magnetic maps that resolve quadrupolar and higher-order features, or when modeling non-uniform and clustered magnetic sources.

For the ground-truth models, the superior performance of SH models observed in our experiments is a direct consequence of their ability to capture the stray fields generated by non-spherical geometries and complex internal magnetic structures. The near-surface stray fields of these models reveal that for grains with significant shape anisotropy or those exhibiting vortex state domains, the dipolar term accounts for only a fraction of the total magnetic signal. The SH inversion is more capable of fitting the higher-order magnetic signals without biasing the degree one coefficients, leading to the significantly lower residuals reported in our benchmarks. Thus, this performance of the tested inversion strategies depends primarily on sensor height and source geometry. For most simulated cases, both nonlinear approaches converged to similar solutions at larger sensor-to-sample distances, whereas discrepancies became more evident for larger grains and at lower heights. This behavior is consistent with the rapid distance-dependent attenuation of higher-order magnetic field components \citep{Bellon2026, CortesOrtuno2022}. As the observation height increases, the external field progressively approaches a dipolar signature, reducing the practical advantage of more flexible source representations. 

However, this behavior should not be generalized to lower sensor heights, where higher-order terms become increasingly relevant. The comparison using real QDM data broadly supports this interpretation. For isolated and well-defined anomalies, both approaches yielded similar solutions, whereas more noticeable differences emerged for weak or externally perturbed signals. Therefore, under standard QDM acquisition conditions, the choice between the two nonlinear approaches may have a limited practical impact for most particle sizes. Although experimental data introduce additional sources of uncertainty not present in the synthetic tests, the same general trends were observed.

\subsection{Euler Deconvolution as a Strategic Seed for Expansion Centers}

The efficacy of SH expansions in magnetic microscopy strongly depends on the accurate definition of the source expansion center. The ED solution provides first-order estimates of the source coordinates $(x_c, y_c, z_c)$ without requiring prior information regarding particle shape or size. By independently constraining source location before coefficient estimation, the original highly nonlinear optimization problem is transformed into a substantially better-conditioned inversion problem. This separation reduces the number of free parameters during coefficient estimation and improves convergence stability for local planar inversions.

In addition, the Euler-derived estimate provides an efficient starting point for subsequent nonlinear refinements. When required, the initial location can be further optimized using derivative-free search algorithms such as the Nelder-Mead method \citep{Nelder-Mead1965}, minimizing local residual RMS values. As demonstrated by the micromagnetic models, this refinement significantly improves solution accuracy and stability, particularly for non-ideal source geometries. Together, these steps create a flexible framework capable of extracting complex magnetic signatures directly from measured anomalies while relying only on a single measurement plane. Consequently, higher-order magnetic characterization can be performed without dependence on auxiliary imaging techniques.

Despite these advantages, some limitations remain. Higher-order spherical harmonic terms become progressively more sensitive to high-frequency noise and positioning uncertainties, particularly at very low sensor-to-sample distances where short-wavelength magnetic components dominate the observed field. As noted by \citet{Lima2023}, increasing the expansion degree beyond the information content of the data can lead to overfitting, where higher-order coefficients begin fitting measurement noise rather than physically meaningful signal components.

The sensitivity of source position estimation is also partially inherited from the ED formulation itself, which relies on directional derivatives of the magnetic field and tends to affect vertical coordinate estimates more strongly. This issue can be mitigated through subsequent nonlinear optimization (Section~\ref{nonlinear-inversion}) or through more robust approaches for determining expansion centers \citep[\textit{e.g.} Euler inversion,][]{Uieda2025}. Additionally, the intrinsic non-uniqueness of multipolar representations implies that different coefficient combinations may generate similar external magnetic fields. Therefore, the final solution may still depend on regularization choices, local anomaly isolation, and the selected maximum expansion degree.

Nevertheless, the ED-seeded approach remains computationally attractive and scalable for large magnetic microscopy datasets. Because the source location is independently constrained prior to coefficient estimation, the dimensionality of the nonlinear optimization problem is substantially reduced. This separation improves numerical stability and makes the method well-suited for the increasingly large and high-resolution datasets generated by modern magnetic microscopy systems.

\subsection{Implications for High-Resolution Paleomagnetic Microscopy}

Since the findings of \citet{Berndt2016}, relatively few magnetic microscopy applications have been directed toward paleomagnetic studies because the number of magnetic grains required to recover statistically reliable paleodirections was considered prohibitively large for these techniques. Nearly a decade later, however, \citet{Bellon2025a} demonstrated through micromagnetic modeling of single-vortex (SV) grains that the number of grains required may be orders of magnitude lower than previously assumed, ranging from several hundred to a few thousand grains for bias fields $\geq 2~\mu T$. These results substantially changed the perspective on the role of magnetic microscopy in paleomagnetism. If reliable directional information can be obtained from much smaller grain populations, the emphasis shifts from measuring large samples toward improving the characterization of individual grains and small particle assemblages.  

This shift naturally motivates the development of magnetic microscopy systems with increasingly higher spatial resolution and progressively smaller sensor-to-sample distances, as observed in recent Quantum Scanning Microscopes (QSM) developments \citep[\textit{e.g.}][]{Ahmed2026}. Such improvements are desirable because they enable the magnetic signal of individual grains and local remanence structures to be resolved with greater fidelity. However, moving closer to the source also places measurements within the near-field regime, where higher-order magnetic contributions become increasingly significant, particularly for non-uniform grains and interacting particle assemblages \citep{Bellon2026, CortesOrtuno2022, CortesOrtuno2021}.  Under these conditions, purely dipolar approaches may no longer provide sufficiently accurate representations of the measured magnetic field. Since multipole terms of degree $m$ scale with $1/r^{m+2}$, the contribution of higher-order components decays progressively faster with distance from the source. Consequently, lower-order terms dominate the far-field behavior, whereas near-field measurements retain substantial contributions from higher-order harmonics. These additional degrees of freedom are required to capture the short-wavelength spatial variations and complex stray-field structures associated with surface-proximal sources.

The framework proposed here directly addresses this emerging regime. By combining spherical harmonic expansions with Euler deconvolution-based estimates of the expansion center, complex magnetic signatures can be modeled with substantially greater stability and physical realism. This approach enables high-resolution magnetic microscopy data to be interpreted beyond the dipolar approximation while remaining computationally practical for large datasets. Consequently, these developments may help bridge the gap between modern magnetic microscopy capabilities and quantitative paleomagnetic applications, particularly for the analysis of small magnetic carriers, complex remanence structures, and weakly magnetized geological materials.


\section{Conclusions}

This study evaluated a spherical harmonic expansion approach to estimate degree-1 coefficients and infer the dipole moment from two-dimensional $B_z$ magnetic microscopy observations, using Euler deconvolution to constrain the expansion center. Synthetic experiments based on micromagnetic models demonstrated that the relative performance of the inversion approaches depends strongly on sensor-to-sample distance and source geometry. At larger distances, solutions progressively converge toward dipolar behavior due to the attenuation of higher-order magnetic contributions, whereas lower sensor heights emphasize complex magnetic structures that require richer source representations. The use of Euler deconvolution as an initial estimate for the expansion center substantially improves inversion conditioning and reduces the dimensionality. When combined with the nonlinear refinement, this approach stably retrieves multipolar source models directly from single observation plane magnetic anomalies. As magnetic microscopy systems continue moving toward higher spatial resolutions and smaller sensor-to-sample distances, measurements increasingly enter a near-field regime where higher-order magnetic signatures become significant components of the observed field. Under these conditions, approaches relying exclusively on dipolar assumptions may become insufficient. The approach presented here provides a physically consistent alternative capable of representing this increased complexity while remaining computationally tractable. These developments may contribute to expanding the application of magnetic microscopy to quantitative paleomagnetic studies, particularly for the investigation of small magnetic carriers, rare geological materials, and complex remanence structures.
